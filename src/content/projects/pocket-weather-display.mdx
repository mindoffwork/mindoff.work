## Working specification

- **Display:** 4.2-inch monochrome e-paper panel with partial-refresh support.
- **Controller:** Low-power microcontroller with Wi-Fi enabled only during updates.
- **Power:** Rechargeable lithium polymer cell, USB-C charging, hardware power switch.
- **Cadence:** Forecast refresh every 45 minutes during daytime and every three hours overnight.
- **Enclosure:** Rounded recycled PETG shell with an inset tabletop stand.

The screen intentionally avoids maps, scrolling detail, and hourly precision
past the current outing. It presents current temperature, feels-like
temperature, a six-hour condition strip, and one plain-language cue such as
`Umbrella likely after 16:00`.

## Designing for the glance

Early screens tried to compress an ordinary weather application into four
inches: sunrise, wind direction, humidity, probability graphs, daily highs,
and icons for everything. The information was accurate but not useful at a
doorway. Finding the relevant instruction took longer than taking out a phone.

The reduced layout begins with the likely decision. Rain probability becomes a
strong band only when it crosses a meaningful threshold. Temperature matters
most when it changes clothing, so unusually warm or cool conditions receive a
short line of text. Wind appears only when it is strong enough to change a
cycle or walk.

E-paper made this restraint visible. Without glow or animation, hierarchy has
to survive through spacing, weight, and language alone.

## Prototype and enclosure

The first enclosure was a foam-board wedge placed beside the key bowl for one
week. That crude model answered a useful question before any printing began:
a low angle reflected overhead light and made the lower half of the panel hard
to read while standing. The final stand holds the face more upright, with
enough rear weight that tapping the update button does not shift it across the
shelf.

The printed enclosure separates into a front bezel and rear service plate.
Four heat-set inserts avoid wearing out plastic threads during battery changes,
and the USB-C port remains exposed at the bottom edge without a loose flap.
The face is unlabelled except for a small embossed charging mark underneath.

## Firmware strategy

Battery life improved more through restraint than through component changes.
On waking, the controller connects, fetches a compact forecast payload, checks
whether any displayed value has materially changed, and refreshes only if
needed. It returns to deep sleep immediately afterward.

A failed update leaves the last successful forecast visible with a subtle
timestamp and connection marker. An empty screen would imply the object had
stopped being useful; stale-but-labelled information is safer and easier to
understand.

## Result

The current prototype sits close to the door and gets used without becoming a
device to maintain. A charge comfortably covers several weeks of ordinary
updates, and the quiet screen fits the room better than another illuminated
panel.

There is still a practical next step: testing the update schedule through a
full season of rapidly changing weather. If the cue language remains helpful
on uncertain days, the design is ready for a smaller enclosure and a custom
circuit board.
