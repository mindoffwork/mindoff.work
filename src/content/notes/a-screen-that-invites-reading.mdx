I used to think a reading screen was finished once the type was comfortable.
Choose a generous measure, give paragraphs enough air, soften the surrounding
interface, and the work was mostly done. Then I tried to read long entries in
an app I had made and noticed how often my eyes wandered away from the
sentence in front of them.

Nothing was dramatically wrong. The toolbar was useful. The progress bar was
accurate. The recommendation rail contained sensible links. Together they
made a small argument that I should keep deciding rather than keep reading.

## The competition around a paragraph

Interface elements do not need to animate or shout in order to compete. A
sticky control remains in view because it expects future use. A row of tags
invites sorting. A share button quietly proposes a public action before a
private thought has even landed.

This is not a case against useful navigation. It is a question of timing.
While I am choosing something to read, context is helpful: length, category,
summary, related pieces. Once I have chosen it, the article should be allowed
to become the principal object on the screen.

I began treating the first viewport as a promise. It should answer only the
questions required to begin:

- What am I reading?
- How much time might it ask of me?
- Is there an image or opening gesture that sets the tone?

Everything else can wait below the essay or remain one calm step away.

## A measure for attention

The layout became narrower, but the more important change was rhythmic.
Headings gained enough separation to mark a turn without becoming billboards.
Paragraphs stopped being interrupted by controls. Reading time moved into the
small line beside the date, where it could be found without becoming a
challenge to finish quickly.

I also removed the false urgency of a percentage-complete indicator. It had
seemed considerate when I built it. In practice it converted an essay into a
task being discharged. A reader who wants to know whether a piece is short can
learn that before starting; a reader already inside it does not need a meter
counting the distance to escape.

> A reading interface is successful when its helpfulness fades at the moment
> attention finds the text.

Images needed the same discipline. A cover can provide a breath before an
article, a change of temperature between title and prose. It does not have to
explain the piece or compress it into a poster. A quiet image leaves a little
interpretive room for the words that follow.

## What remains visible

Restraint is not the same as disappearance. I kept a clear route back to the
notes index because orientation is kindness. I kept date and reading time
because they help a reader decide whether this is the right moment. I kept
tags at the end because a thought can open outward after it has been read.

The distinction is simple: controls that help someone enter or leave should
be dependable; controls that suggest what to do instead should not occupy the
reading itself.

## The test I trust

I now test a reading layout by putting a real essay into it and staying there
for ten minutes. Pixel inspection reveals spacing mistakes, but only reading
reveals interruptions. If I catch myself admiring the interface, checking my
progress, or planning my next click, there is still something asking to be
made quieter.

Good reading design is not empty design. It is full of decisions made in
support of an experience that does not keep announcing those decisions. The
screen opens the door, turns on a steady light, and then lets the page speak.
