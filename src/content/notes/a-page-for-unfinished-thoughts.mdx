Most ideas arrive before they are ready to explain themselves. A line heard on
a walk, an awkward interaction in a product, a material combination worth
testing: each is clear enough to notice and too unfinished to turn into a
finished piece of work.

I used to lose these thoughts in one of two ways. I ignored them until they
vanished, or I gave them a title and a destination immediately. The first
discarded useful curiosity. The second asked a small observation to behave like
a commitment.

## A page with no promise

Now I keep a single page for fragments. Each entry is brief: what I noticed,
where it appeared, and one question it might be asking. There are no labels for
urgency and no obligation to return. Its purpose is not to protect every idea;
it is to make enough room for the interesting ones to repeat themselves.

Repeated thoughts earn a second look. A sentence that keeps returning may
become an essay. A minor interface frustration encountered three times may
become a project change. Most fragments never move beyond the page, and that
is useful too. They were observations, not debts.

## Leaving room

An unfinished thought is often valuable precisely because it has not chosen a
form. Keeping it small preserves the possibility that it wants to become
something other than my first assumption: a sketch instead of a feature, a
conversation instead of an article, or nothing more than a better way of
seeing the afternoon.

The page is less an archive than a clearing. It reminds me that noticing is
worth doing even when production does not immediately follow.
