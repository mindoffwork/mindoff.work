Rain had ended just before I reached the stop. The shelter was still ticking
with water from its metal edge, and the road carried the color of every sign
above it: pharmacy green, tea-stall yellow, the red brake lights of a bus
turning toward us from two streets away.

There were five passengers when I boarded. Nobody spoke. One person held a
folded umbrella carefully between their shoes; another slept with a grocery
bag balanced against the window. The driver kept the cabin lights low, so the
glass became a moving double image of the street and the few faces inside.

## A familiar road changed by water

This route is usually pure function. I know where traffic compresses, which
turn leans hard enough to move a backpack, and how early to stand before my
stop. That evening the familiar markers were still there, but the wet road
gave each of them a second life below the wheels.

A row of closed shutters stretched downward in wavering silver lines. Scooters
passed through shallow puddles without hurry. At a junction, a fruit seller
was tying a sheet over his cart, the oranges under it lit for a moment like a
small interior room.

I had no camera ready and was glad of it. Some views are improved by not asking
them to hold still. They stay attached to movement: wipers crossing at an
uneven tempo, a window fogging near someone's shoulder, the bell for a stop
pressed once and answered by the softest change in engine pitch.

## Getting off

My street was nearly dry. The bus pulled away before I opened the umbrella,
leaving a brief wake of reflected light that broke apart as the road settled.
By the time I reached home, the ride had already resumed its ordinary scale:
twenty minutes, one fare, a route taken often.

But ordinary travel sometimes gives back more than transport. It makes a
known place unfamiliar just long enough to see it again.
