The city felt half-drawn in pencil: soft air, narrow lanes, shuttered shops,
and the occasional bicycle moving as if it had permission from the morning.
Rain had passed before dawn and left every stone holding a small version of
the sky.

I had planned to photograph a landmark. Instead I kept stopping for the
ordinary parts of the street: a lantern still lit beside a wooden door, a
basket on a parked bicycle, a vending machine making a blue rectangle in the
quiet. Nothing asked to be documented, which made looking feel less like
collecting and more like paying attention.

## Before the itinerary

At six, a travelled place briefly stops performing its identity. Deliveries
have not begun; shop curtains have not risen; the route described in a guide
book is simply a lane where somebody swept yesterday's leaves to one side.
The most memorable sound was not a temple bell but tyres slipping over damp
stone and then becoming quiet again.

There is a useful lesson in walking before the day has an agenda. Attention is
easier when I am not asking each scene to become evidence of a good trip. I can
notice light pooling on cedar, the careful interval between doorways, or the
way the mountains become visible only where the roofs allow them.

By breakfast, the street was busier and brighter. It was no less beautiful,
only less available for lingering. The early walk stayed with me because it
did not produce a list of sights. It gave me one unhurried hour in which a
place could remain a place.
